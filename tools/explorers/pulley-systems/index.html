1 · Rope constraint

Use one shared vertical coordinate convention, positive downward for both masses:

Δy₁ + Δy₂ = 0  ⇒  a₁ = −a₂

This equality comes from fixed rope length. It is not a Newton's-third-law result.

Why the tension sits there

Ideal-rope tension is equal along the same massless rope over an ideal pulley. That is an idealization, not a statement that T = mg.

One mass moves twice as far — but why?

Drag the movable pulley vertically, or use the slider. Downward is positive for both x₁ and x₂.

Rope-length accounting

Only three straight segments change length. The curved contact lengths around ideal pulleys remain constant.

L = 2x₂ + x₁ + constant

right support+0.0 cm
free segment0.0 cm

2Δx₂ + Δx₁ = 0
v₁ = −2v₂     a₁ = −2a₂
Magnitude: |a₁| = 2|a₂|. Direction: the accelerations are opposite.

This 2:1 result belongs to this specific rope configuration. It is not a universal Atwood-machine rule.

Force / displacement tradeoff

Two rope segments pull upward on the movable pulley, so the ideal support is 2T. The free end moves twice the load displacement.

free-end distance = 2 × load distance
ideal quasi-static comparison: Fin(2d) ≈ Fload(d)

The work correspondence above is the ideal force-advantage picture for holding or quasi-static motion. During acceleration, kinetic energy changes, so do not replace the full dynamics with that work equality.

2T = mg

Holding / quasi-static case: a = 0. The two upward tensions exactly balance the load weight.

mg − 2T = ma

Downward is positive. Therefore T = m(g − a)/2 while the rope remains taut.

Guardrail: 2T = mg is not universal. It is the zero-acceleration case of the signed dynamics equation.

Physics guardrails used by this page

  • Simple-Atwood equal-and-opposite accelerations come from fixed rope length, not Newton's third law.
  • The 2:1 movable-pulley relation is configuration-specific.
  • Equal tension along one ideal massless rope over ideal pulleys is an idealization.
  • Signed equations always state the positive direction.
  • Static support equations are not reused as accelerating-load equations.
